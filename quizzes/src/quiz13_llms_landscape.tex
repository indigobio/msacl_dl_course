% =========================== Q1 ===========================
\textbf{1. Place a tool you've never seen.}\; {\small Not a match --- a \textbf{transfer}. Using the
five-tool landscape map from the slides, place each new tool by its \textbf{architecture family} and the
\textbf{landmark it most resembles}.}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{(a)} A tool reads a routine \textbf{MALDI-TOF spectrum} and calls the bacterial \textbf{species}
(not resistance).\\[2pt]
\hspace*{1em}architecture family $\rightarrow$ \rb{1D CNN (spectrum classifier)}{5.2cm}\quad
most like $\rightarrow$ \rb{DRIAMS}{2.6cm}
\work{0.4cm}

\textbf{(b)} A tool reads a \textbf{peptide sequence} and predicts its \textbf{fragment spectrum + retention
time} for a library.\\[2pt]
\hspace*{1em}architecture family $\rightarrow$ \rb{deep sequence model (RNN/attention)}{5.2cm}\quad
most like $\rightarrow$ \rb{Prosit}{2.6cm}
\work{0.4cm}

\textbf{(c)} \textbf{Casanovo} (spectrum $\rightarrow$ peptide) and \textbf{AlphaFold} (sequence $\rightarrow$
3D fold) solve very different problems but share \emph{one} architecture family. Which one, and what do their
problems have in common that makes it fit?

\work{1.0cm}
\ifsolution\else\blk{\linewidth}\\[6pt]\blk{\linewidth}\fi

\keybox{(a) \textbf{1D CNN} (a spectrum-in, one-label-out classifier), most like \textbf{DRIAMS} --- the same
architecture, just a species call instead of R/S. (b) \textbf{Deep sequence model} (RNN/LSTM + attention, or a
transformer), most like \textbf{Prosit} --- sequence-in, predicted-spectrum/RT-out. (c) Both use a
\textbf{Transformer / attention}; their problems share \textbf{long-range dependencies across a sequence} ---
distant peaks (Casanovo) or distant residues (AlphaFold) must inform each other, exactly what attention
captures, whereas a CNN sees only local windows.}

\vspace{2pt}\hrule\vspace{5pt}

% =========================== Q2 ===========================
\textbf{2. Label the reinforcement-learning loop.}\; {\small An optimizer \textbf{auto-tunes an LC gradient}
to maximize peak separation. Each cycle it \emph{picks a new gradient slope}, \emph{runs the sample}, and
\emph{scores the resulting chromatogram's resolution}; it keeps the settings that score best. Fill in each
role of the RL loop.}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{(a)} The \textbf{AGENT} (the learner) is\hfill $\rightarrow$\;\rb{the tuning optimizer}{5.6cm}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(b)} The \textbf{ACTION} it takes each cycle is\hfill $\rightarrow$\;\rb{picking a gradient slope}{5.6cm}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(c)} The \textbf{REWARD} it learns from is\hfill $\rightarrow$\;\rb{the chromatogram's resolution score}{5.6cm}

\work{0.4cm}

\keybox{\textbf{(a) AGENT = the tuning optimizer} --- the learner that chooses settings.\;
\textbf{(b) ACTION = the gradient it picks} that cycle (e.g.\ the slope/steps).\;
\textbf{(c) REWARD = the chromatogram's resolution / separation score} (higher is better).
(The \emph{environment} is the LC--MS instrument + sample.) Just like AlphaGo, no one labels the ``right''
gradient --- the optimizer discovers it by trying gradients and reading the reward. This is also exactly
how RLHF tunes an LLM, with a \emph{human preference} as the reward.}

\vspace{2pt}\hrule\vspace{5pt}

% =========================== Q3 ===========================
\textbf{3. Prompting, RAG, or fine-tuning?}\; {\small You want the assistant to \textbf{always format results
in your lab's exact report style} --- the same structure every time --- and you have \textbf{2{,}000 past
reports} as examples.}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(a)} Which approach fits best --- \emph{prompting}, \emph{RAG} (retrieval-augmented generation), or
\emph{fine-tuning}?\hfill $\rightarrow$\;\rb{fine-tuning}{3.4cm}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(b)} In one sentence, why?

\work{1.0cm}
\ifsolution\else\blk{\linewidth}\fi

\keybox{(a) \textbf{Fine-tuning.} (b) You want a \textbf{consistent new style applied every time} and you have
\textbf{many labeled examples} (2{,}000 reports) --- exactly the ``consistent new style/skill + many
examples'' case, so \textbf{bake the style into the weights} by fine-tuning. \textbf{RAG} is for retrieving
\emph{changing facts} (here the content varies but the style is fixed); \textbf{plain prompting} can't
reliably enforce one house style across every report from a few in-prompt examples. (Contrast the in-class
example: a report that must quote \emph{today's SOP} $\rightarrow$ RAG, because that was about changing facts.)}

\vspace{2pt}\hrule\vspace{5pt}

% =========================== Q4 ===========================
\textbf{4. Spot what you can't trust.}\; {\small An LLM answers a question with: \emph{``It's 48 nmol/L ---
see Nguyen et al., Clin.\ Chem.\ 2021; 67:1043--1051.''} It sounds fluent and confident.}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(a)} Which part(s) of this answer can you \textbf{not trust} without checking a source?

\work{0.8cm}
\ifsolution\else\blk{\linewidth}\fi

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{(b)} \emph{Why} did the model state them so confidently?

\work{0.8cm}
\ifsolution\else\blk{\linewidth}\fi

\keybox{(a) \textbf{Both specifics}: the \textbf{number} (48 nmol/L) \emph{and} the \textbf{citation} (Nguyen
et al., Clin.\ Chem.\ 2021). You cannot tell from the fluent wording whether either is real --- any specific
fact an LLM emits \emph{without a cited source} is unverified by construction. (b) Because an LLM is trained
to produce \textbf{plausible next tokens, not verified truth}, so a fabricated number or paper comes out with
the \emph{same} confidence as a real one --- \emph{hallucination}. The rule: treat every LLM-produced specific
(number, citation, dose) as a \textbf{draft to verify} --- ground it in RAG'd sources, require a human
sign-off --- never as the record.}
