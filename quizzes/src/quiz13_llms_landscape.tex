% =========================== Q1 ===========================
\textbf{1. Formulate one of your own problems as reinforcement learning.}\;
{\small Pick a decision that gets made \emph{over and over} in your own lab --- tuning a method, scheduling QC or
calibration, choosing which flagged sample to re-run, deciding what to acquire next --- and write it as an RL
problem. There is no single right answer; what matters is that the four roles are coherent and that the reward
really encodes what you want.}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{(a)} The problem, in one line:\rbo{3pt}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(b)} \textbf{AGENT} --- who or what decides?\rbo{3pt}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(c)} \textbf{ACTIONS} --- what may it change?\rbo{3pt}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(d)} \textbf{STATE} --- what does it observe before deciding?\rbo{3pt}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(e)} \textbf{REWARD} --- what number says the outcome was better?\rbo{3pt}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(f)} \emph{Is your state \textbf{Markov}} --- is it enough on its own to decide from, without knowing how
you got there? \emph{If not, what would you add?}\rbo{3pt}\rbo{8pt}

\vspace{2pt}

\keybox{\textbf{Open-ended --- mark the reasoning, not the answer.} A good response has four coherent roles, e.g.
\emph{auto-tuning an LC gradient}: \textbf{agent} = the tuning routine; \textbf{actions} = the gradient changes it
may make; \textbf{state} = the current settings plus the last run's separation metrics; \textbf{reward} = a
resolution or peak-capacity score, minus instrument time. Three things to look for. (1) \textbf{Is it really RL?}
If you already know the correct action for every case, you need a \emph{classifier}, not RL --- RL earns its keep
only when nobody can label the right action and the feedback arrives afterwards. (2) \textbf{Does the reward
encode what you want?} ``Maximise identifications'' is gamed by loosening the FDR; a reward must carry the cost as
well as the benefit (\emph{reward hacking}). (3) \textbf{Is the state Markov?} A single instrument reading usually
is \emph{not} --- add the rate of drift, the position in the run, or the last few readings, and it usually is. The
fix for a non-Markov problem is always the \textbf{state}, never the algorithm.}

\vspace{4pt}

% =========================== Q2 ===========================
\textbf{2. Prompting, RAG, or fine-tuning?}\; {\small You want the assistant to \textbf{always format results
in your lab's exact report style} --- the same structure every time --- and you have \textbf{2{,}000 past
reports} as examples.}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(a)} Which approach fits best --- \emph{prompting}, \emph{RAG} (retrieval-augmented generation), or
\emph{fine-tuning}?\\[2pt]
\hspace*{1em}$\rightarrow$\;\rb{fine-tuning}{4cm}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(b)} In one sentence, why?

\work{1.0cm}
\ifsolution\else\blk{\linewidth}\fi

\keybox{(a) \textbf{Fine-tuning.} (b) You want a \textbf{consistent new style applied every time} and you have
\textbf{many labeled examples} (2{,}000 reports) --- exactly the ``consistent new style/skill + many
examples'' case, so \textbf{bake the style into the weights} by fine-tuning. \textbf{RAG} is for retrieving
\emph{changing facts} (here the content varies but the style is fixed); \textbf{plain prompting} can't
reliably enforce one house style across every report from a few in-prompt examples. (Contrast the in-class
example: a report that must quote \emph{today's SOP} $\rightarrow$ RAG, because that was about changing facts.)}

\vspace{2pt}\hrule\vspace{5pt}

% =========================== Q3 ===========================
\textbf{3. Spot what you can't trust.}\; {\small An LLM answers a question with: \emph{``It's 48 nmol/L ---
see Nguyen et al., Clin.\ Chem.\ 2021; 67:1043--1051.''} It sounds fluent and confident.}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(a)} Which part(s) of this answer can you \textbf{not trust} without checking a source?

\work{0.8cm}
\ifsolution\else\blk{\linewidth}\fi

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{(b)} \emph{Why} did the model state them so confidently?

\work{0.8cm}
\ifsolution\else\blk{\linewidth}\fi

\keybox{(a) \textbf{Both specifics}: the \textbf{number} (48 nmol/L) \emph{and} the \textbf{citation} (Nguyen
et al., Clin.\ Chem.\ 2021). You cannot tell from the fluent wording whether either is real --- any specific
fact an LLM emits \emph{without a cited source} is unverified by construction. (b) Because an LLM is trained
to produce \textbf{plausible next tokens, not verified truth}, so a fabricated number or paper comes out with
the \emph{same} confidence as a real one --- \emph{hallucination}. The rule: treat every LLM-produced specific
(number, citation, dose) as a \textbf{draft to verify} --- ground it in RAG'd sources, require a human
sign-off --- never as the record.}

% =========================== Q4 ===========================
\textbf{4. Explore or exploit?}\; {\small An agent chooses between two \textbf{routes to work} each morning and scores
each trip (higher is better). So far: route \textbf{A} has been driven \textbf{30 times} for a total score of
\textbf{180}; route \textbf{B} has been driven \textbf{twice} for a total of \textbf{8}. It uses \textbf{$\varepsilon$-greedy} with $\varepsilon = 0.2$ over $K = 2$ actions: with
probability $\varepsilon$ pick a random action, otherwise pick the highest average so far --- so
$P(\text{a specific non-greedy action}) = \varepsilon / K$.}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(a)} Which route does the \textbf{greedy} rule take? $\rightarrow$\ \rb{A (mean $6.0$ vs $4.0$)}{5.5cm}
\work{0.3cm}

\textbf{(b)} What is the probability that \emph{this} trip takes \textbf{B}? $\rightarrow$\
\rb{$0.2 / 2 = 0.10$}{4cm}
\work{0.3cm}

\textbf{(c)} \emph{One sentence: why would an \textbf{always-greedy} driver be a mistake here?}

\work{0.8cm}
\ifsolution\else\blk{\linewidth}\fi

\keybox{(a) \textbf{A}: its average is $180/30 = \mathbf{6.0}$ against B's $8/2 = \mathbf{4.0}$, and
``greedy'' means \emph{highest average so far}. (b) $P(B) = \varepsilon/K = 0.2/2 = \mathbf{0.10}$ ---
about 1 cycle in 10. (Not $0.2$: that is the chance of exploring \emph{at all}, and half those draws land
back on A.) (c) Any equivalent of: \textbf{B has only been driven twice}, so its $4.0$ is nearly meaningless
--- two bad days of traffic would produce it --- and an always-greedy driver would take A forever and never
collect the evidence that would settle which route is faster.}

\vspace{4pt}

% =========================== Q5 ===========================
\textbf{5. The reward arrives late.}\; {\small An agent takes three steps through the gridworld, earning rewards
$r_1 = 0$, $r_2 = 0$, $r_3 = 1$ --- only the last step reaches the goal. The \textbf{return} credits a step
with the rewards that \emph{follow} it, discounted one notch per step:
$G = r_1 + \gamma\, r_2 + \gamma^2 r_3$.}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(a)} With $\gamma = 0.5$, how much credit does \textbf{step 1} get? $\rightarrow$\
\rb{$0 + 0.5{\times}0 + 0.25{\times}1 = 0.25$}{6cm}
\work{0.3cm}

\textbf{(b)} \emph{One sentence: what does a \textbf{small} $\gamma$ make the agent do?}

\work{0.8cm}
\ifsolution\else\blk{\linewidth}\fi

\keybox{(a) $G = 0 + 0.5{\times}0 + 0.5^2{\times}1 = 0.25 \times 1 = \mathbf{0.25}$ (compare $\gamma = 0.9$,
worked in class, which gives $0.9^2 = \mathbf{0.81}$). (b) A small $\gamma$ makes the agent \textbf{myopic}
--- it heavily discounts anything more than a step or two away, so it chases immediate reward and barely
credits the early moves that set up a late win. For a long episode --- a method development, or a game of Go
--- you want $\gamma$ near 1.}
