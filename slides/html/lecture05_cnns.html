Why CNN · 1 of 4

A fully connected layer doesn't scale to images

One modest photo, one layer: 30 million weights — each one needing data to learn it.

Your MALDI-TOF spectrum has the same problem in 1D: 6,000 bins into that layer is 6 million weights, and the MLP must relearn "what a peak looks like" separately at every position.
The motivating example is a plain photo — the classic doorway to CNNs. Every pixel wired to every neuron; the waste isn't just memory, every weight needs training data. Callback to Lab 1: our MLP was fine there because 189 metabolite features have no spatial order — pixels do. Three observations about images fix this, and each transfers verbatim to spectra at the end of the section.
Why CNN · 2 of 4

Property 1 — patterns are local

To recognize a beak, a neuron doesn't need the whole bird — a small window is enough.

So connect neurons to windows, not to every pixel.

Exactly as true of your data: a peak lives in a narrow m/z window — the rest of the spectrum is baseline.
Hung-yi Lee's beak detector, told on the image itself. Consequence: connect each detector neuron to a small window instead of everything → far fewer weights. Then land the MS beat via the callout — a peak is every bit as local in a spectrum.
Why CNN · 3 of 4

Property 2 — the same pattern appears anywhere

An "upper-left beak detector" and a "lower-right beak detector" would do almost the same job.

So make them share one set of weights — one detector, slid everywhere.

A Gaussian peak at m/z 300 looks just like one at m/z 4,800 — one peak detector, slid along the whole axis.
This is weight sharing — the single most important idea of the hour, and what "convolution" will mean mechanically in a minute. One beak detector reused at every position instead of one per location; the callout lands the same move for spectra: one peak-shape detector for all 6,000 bins.
Why CNN · 4 of 4

Property 3 — downsampling doesn't change what it is

One pixel kept out of every twenty: same bird — and the next layer gets far less to process.

This will justify pooling in a few slides.

Downsample 6,000 bins to 1,500: the spectrum is still unmistakably itself.
Subsampling the pixels does not change the object — cheaper for the next layer, identity preserved; this is the license for pooling later. Wrap the section: three properties → three design moves — local windows, shared weights, pooling — and each held for spectra too (the callouts). Now we compute all three by hand.
Convolution by hand · setup

A filter is a tiny pattern detector

the 9 filter values are learned weights — training discovers what to hunt for
Grids straight from Hung-yi Lee's tutorial. The image is 0/1 for arithmetic comfort. Read the filter aloud: +1 down the diagonal, −1 elsewhere — it will "light up" wherever the image has a diagonal streak. Emphasize: WE won't choose these values in practice; gradient descent (Lecture 2!) learns them. Today we hand-pick them so we can compute.
Convolution by hand · step 1

Overlay, multiply, add — that's the whole operation

Walk the nine products slowly — this is the only full dot product we do out loud. Six are zero; the three 1×1 hits are exactly the diagonal matching. Result 3 = strong match. This number is a neuron's weighted sum from Lecture 1 — same operation, arranged spatially.
Convolution by hand · step 2

Slide one step — stride 1

stride = how far the window jumps each step
Same nine weights, new window — this is weight sharing happening before their eyes. −1 = weak match here. Keep momentum; don't recompute every term aloud.

Convolution by hand · result

The feature map is a where-is-my-pattern map

large values = "diagonal found here" · 36 pixels → 16 responses, using only 9 weights
Point at the two 3s — the image really does have diagonal streaks starting there. Contrast with the MLP from slide 3: nine shared weights replaced thousands. The feature map is itself an image — which is why we'll be able to stack another convolution on top of it.
Convolution by hand · stride

Stride 2: bigger jumps, smaller map

same weights, half the stops in each direction → a quarter of the outputs
Stride is a resolution/cost dial. They will turn this dial themselves in Lab 2's hyperparameter play.
Convolution by hand · padding

Zero padding keeps the edges in play

without padding the map shrinks every layer — and edge pixels get fewer looks
One idea, one slide: ring of zeros → output size matches input. In spectra terms: without padding, the peaks at the very start and end of your m/z range are under-observed.
Convolution by hand · channels

Every filter gets its own map — maps stack into channels

a conv layer with 32 filters turns one image into a 32-channel stack of pattern maps
Filter 2 hunts vertical strokes; the image barely has any, so responses stay low — filters specialize. "Channels" finally defined: the depth axis of the stack. An RGB input already arrives as 3 channels; filters then see all channels at once (mention only, don't compute).

Convolution by hand · pooling

Max pooling: keep each region's strongest response

property 3 in action: half the size, the "diagonal found" story intact — and shifted-by-one-pixel inputs now give the same answer
Pooling has no weights — nothing to learn, purely mechanical. Two gifts: cheaper next layer, and tolerance to tiny shifts (a peak wobbling one bin left doesn't change the pooled answer). This is exactly why calibration jitter doesn't wreck CNNs on spectra.

Convolution by hand · the one formula

How big is the output? One formula, no calculus

out = (n − k + 2p) / s + 1
our stride-1 walk:  (6 − 3 + 0)/1 + 1 = 4 ✓
with padding 1:  (6 − 3 + 2)/1 + 1 = 6 ✓
Quiz 5 asks you to run this formula once — and to fill in feature-map cells on grids just like today's.
The diagram makes all four letters concrete before the formula runs: n is the plain input width (no padding counted), p is the ring added on each side, k is the filter window's own width, and s is how far that window jumps between the amber and teal positions shown. Verify the formula against the maps we just built — it should feel like bookkeeping, not math. This is the only formula of the hour.

1D · in PyTorch

You already know every argument

Deeper · what "just stack more" looks like

Inside VGG: your grid tricks, stacked sixteen deep

every filter is 3×3 — the size you computed by hand · channels grow 64 → 128 → 256 → 512 as pooling shrinks the maps · ≈ 138M weights
Fast beat — 60 seconds, part of the ~6-minute architecture story; don't walk every block. One point: there is nothing new here. Conv (their 6×6 grid), pooling (their max-pool), a fully connected head (Lab 1) — repeated 16 layers deep, channels doubling as maps shrink. The uniform all-3×3 design is why VGG became the "just stack more" poster child, and 138M weights is the cliffhanger's setup: stacking further stopped working — advance to ResNet for why and the fix.
Deeper · the fix

ResNet: give the signal a shortcut

The gradient now has a highway straight back through the network — no more vanishing whisper.

Each block only learns a small correction to what flows through.

1D ResNets are now the standard workhorse for spectra — including the MALDI-TOF resistance models this course builds on.
Payoff of the Lecture 4 setup. 152 layers, better-than-human ImageNet accuracy in 2015. "Skip connection" will also reappear inside transformers on Monday — plant that flag.
Lecture 5 · part four

Where, not just what

object detection — and why it's secretly peak picking
Final 12 minutes. Classification answers "what is this?" — but a lot of lab work is "where are the things?" The divider art already gives it away: boxes on a chromatogram.
Detection · the idea

Object detection = boxes + confidence scores

The CNN backbone is the one you just built. Only the last layers change: instead of one label, predict box positions + confidences.

Names you'll meet: YOLO (fast, one pass) · Faster R-CNN (propose, then verify).

Keep it at this altitude — no anchor boxes, no NMS, no loss functions. The message: detection is a small change of output head on the same convolutional machinery.
Detection · your version of it

A chromatogram is an image whose objects are peaks

The manual step your lab calls peak review is, to a CNN, a detection problem it already knows how to solve.
The reframe of the segment. Every element maps: boxes are integration windows, scores are reviewer confidence, class could be "real peak vs. noise". Someone in the room integrates peaks by hand every week — speak to them.
