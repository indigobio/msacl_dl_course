How does a network learn?

Loss, gradient descent, backpropagation — and the PyTorch training loop

Recap · where Lecture 1 left off

Last hour: a network runs forward. But who set the weights?

A neuron does three things: multiply inputs by weights, add them up, bend through an activation. A network is just that, stacked.

Lecture 1 assumed the weights. Real networks start with random weights and are terrible. Today: how they get good.
Two minutes. Rebuild the bridge before adding anything new. Point at the diagram: this is the Lecture 1 network — same two inputs, one hidden layer, one output — except every weight now reads "w?". Today · the whole idea in one loop

Learning = guess → measure → correct, over and over

These four steps are the PyTorch training loop you will read at the end of the hour. Everything in between is us explaining each box.
Give them the map before the details, so nobody gets lost in the arithmetic later. Walk the loop once, slowly: (1) push an input forward and get a guess; (2) measure how wrong the guess is — that number is the loss; (3) send the blame backward to every weight — that is backpropagation; (4) nudge each weight a little in the improving direction — that is the step. Then repeat, thousands of times. Promise them the payoff slide: these four boxes will reappear as five lines of real PyTorch, and they will recognise every one. This slide is worth memorising; the rest of the hour just fills it in.
Today · the loop you already know

Sighting in a catapult: miss, measure, adjust

You would never solve the catapult with equations. You fire, measure the miss, adjust the power, and fire again — closing in on the target.

A network learns the very same way. The miss is the loss; the power is a weight; adjust and repeat is training. That is the whole hour.
A homely analogy to demystify the whole loop before any math — everyone has an intuition for aiming. You are sighting in a catapult with no physics: you launch, see where the stone lands, measure how far off the target you are, and turn the shooting power up or down accordingly — too short, add power; too far, ease off — then fire again, tightening on the bullseye each shot. Map it explicitly onto the four boxes: the launch is the forward pass, the gap between where it lands and the target is the loss, deciding which way to turn the power dial is the gradient (which direction reduces the miss), and turning it is the step. A neural network does exactly this, just with millions of "power dials" (weights) instead of one. Keep this picture in your pocket; every abstract term today has a catapult twin.
Lecture 2 · part one

Loss: how wrong are we?

before you can fix a mistake, you have to measure it
Transition to box 2. The one idea: to improve, the network needs a single number that says how wrong it currently is. That number is the loss, and driving it down is the entire objective of training. Keep energy up — this is the easy, intuitive part before we earn the backprop math.
Loss · the "how wrong" number

Loss = distance between guess and truth

mean squared error

Why squared? It makes every gap positive, and it punishes a big miss far more than a small one.

One number for the whole dataset: small = good, big = bad. Training just tries to shrink it.
Ten minutes for the whole loss section; keep this to four. Define loss plainly: it scores how far the guesses are from the truth, collapsed to one number. Use MSE because it is the most intuitive — literally the average squared gap. Two reasons for the square, both on the slide: it kills the sign (a miss of −3 and +3 should both count as badness), and it disproportionately penalises large misses, so the network cares most about its worst mistakes. Do NOT introduce cross-entropy — that is Lecture 4, for classification; say so in one sentence so nobody thinks MSE is the only loss. The takeaway to land: loss is the single dial we will turn down for the rest of the hour.
Lecture 2 · part two

Gradient descent

roll downhill on the loss surface, one small step at a time
Box 4, previewed before box 3 — because the "which way is downhill" intuition motivates why we bother computing gradients at all. The ball on the curve is the mental model for the whole section: the height is the loss, the position is the weights, and we want the bottom.
Gradient descent · the ball on the hill

Downhill = the direction that lowers the loss

At any weight setting, the slope of the loss tells us which way is uphill. Go the other way and the loss drops.

how far we move = the step size · the learning rate (lr) is the dial that scales it
The gradient is just the slope of the loss for each weight; the minus sign means "go downhill." How big a step we take is the step size — up next.
This is the engine of all of deep learning, stated with zero calculus. Height = loss, horizontal = a weight's value. The slope at the ball's feet points uphill; subtract a bit of it and you move downhill, toward lower loss. Read the update rule aloud twice — new weight = old weight − learning rate × slope — because it is literally the last line of the training loop and question 2 on the quiz. Introduce the intuitive term first: the amount we move each time is the "step size"; the "learning rate" is simply the dial that sets that step size (it multiplies the slope) — the next slide names it formally. "Gradient" is just our fancy word for "the slope of the loss with respect to a weight"; keep translating it back to "slope" every time. The one honesty note: real loss surfaces live in millions of dimensions, not one — but the picture is faithful, we just can't draw a million axes.

Extended knowledge · a side note, not on the exam

Slope tells you which way · curvature tells you how sharp

Gradient = the slope: which way is downhill and how steep. Curvature (the "second derivative") = how fast that slope is itself changing — how sharply the valley bends.

Newton's method uses curvature to pick a smarter step: a tight, narrow valley → take small steps; a broad, flat one → take big steps. Fewer steps to the bottom.
In deep learning we mostly skip it — curvature is far too expensive across millions of weights. But Adam (Lecture 4) cheaply approximates this idea. The main thread continues on the next slide — this one is optional.
Enrichment slide — flagged optional; PhDs will enjoy it, and it can be skipped if time is short. The whole point is the contrast between the two derivatives, both stated as "slopes of slopes," no calculus machinery. The gradient (first derivative) is the tangent line: it points downhill and tells you how steep the ground is right here — that is all plain gradient descent uses. Curvature (the second derivative) measures how the slope itself changes as you move — how tightly the bowl bends; the dashed red arc is a circle hugging the valley, tighter bend = more curvature. Newton's method exploits it: near a sharply curving minimum you should tiptoe, near a gently curving one you can stride, so it effectively chooses the step size for you and often converges in far fewer steps. Why we do not just use it: computing curvature for millions of weights (a giant matrix) is prohibitively expensive, so deep learning sticks with gradients — but the adaptive optimiser Adam, coming in Lecture 4, is a cheap stand-in that scales the step per weight using recent gradient history. Land it as: gradient = direction, curvature = how the direction is changing; today we only need the first.
Gradient descent · the learning rate

Learning rate = step size: too small, too big, just right

too big

just right

Same downhill rule, three step sizes. The learning rate is the single most important dial you will tune all week.
The learning rate is the multiplier on the slope in the update rule — how big a step we take each time. Walk the three bowls left to right. Too small: the steps are so tiny the ball crawls; training wastes hours, and — because each step can only inch downhill — it can settle into a shallow local dip (a local optimum) and never reach the true bottom. A healthy step size can roll right over such small dips. Too big: each step overshoots the bottom and lands higher up the far wall; the loss bounces around and can even diverge to infinity. Just right: brisk, stable steps that settle into the valley. Tell them plainly this is the first knob they will reach for in every lab and every real project — and that the next slide shows how to read the loss curve to tell which case you are in. Set up the pair prompt.
Gradient descent · your turn

Three training runs — match each to its learning rate

Turn to a neighbour — 2 minutes. One rate is too big, one too small, one just right. Which run is which — and what in each curve gives it away?
This is the assessed think-pair-share for Lecture 2 (the 💬 in the spec). Give them two minutes, then harvest. Answers: Run A's rate is too big — the loss zig-zags violently and trends upward instead of settling, the tell-tale of overshooting the valley (the "too big" bowl from the last slide, seen over time). Run C's rate is too small — the loss inches down so gently it is still high and still falling at the end; it would get there eventually but wastes a lot of compute. Run B is just right — a smooth drop that flattens low as it nears the bottom. Draw the explicit link back to the three bowls: a good run's loss curve looks like the ball rolling calmly to the valley floor, a too-small rate like it barely creeping, a too-big rate like it ricocheting off the walls. Lecture 2 · part three · the heart of the hour

Backpropagation

how each weight learns its share of the blame — worked by hand
The centre of the lecture. Reassure: gradient descent told us to step downhill, but on a network with many weights we need the slope for EACH weight. Backpropagation is the bookkeeping that gets all of them at once. We will do it on the smallest possible network — one input, one hidden neuron, one output, just two weights — with real numbers, so the whole mechanism fits on a slide and every person can check the arithmetic. Go slow here; this is the payoff.
Backprop · the smallest network · forward pass

One input → one hidden → one output: two weights

Forward pass done: we guessed 1.5, the truth was 1, so we are off by 0.5 and the loss is 0.25. Now: how much is each weight to blame?
Set the stage carefully — every number here is reused for three slides and on the quiz. Input x = 2. First weight w1 = 0.5, so the hidden pre-activation is 0.5×2 = 1, positive, so ReLU passes it: h = 1. Second weight w2 = 1.5, so the guess is 1.5×1 = 1.5. The truth is 1, so we overshot by 0.5, and squaring gives loss 0.25. Say it out loud: this is just Lecture 1's forward pass with a loss bolted on the end. No biases and one neuron per layer purely so the math stays checkable by hand. Pose the driving question of the next two slides: the guess was too high — which weight do we blame, and by how much? Have them predict: both weights are positive and the guess is too high, so both should come DOWN.

"Local effect" = the slope at one arrow: how much its output moves when its input moves. Chain them together and you get the loss's slope for a weight — its gradient. Multiplying slopes along a path is exactly the chain rule from calculus — no symbols needed, just multiply.
This is the conceptual key; do not rush it. Backpropagation sounds fearsome but it is one move repeated: to find how much the loss cares about a given weight, walk the path from the loss back to that weight and multiply one small "local slope" at each arrow. Two starting slopes are the same for every weight: the loss's slope with respect to the error is 2×error (the slope of a squared-error bowl is twice the error — that is our only "derivative = slope" fact today), and the error tracks the guess one-for-one, slope 1. What differs per weight is the rest of the path — that is what the next two slides fill in. Emphasise: no calculus, just "how much does the output move if the input moves" at each step, all multiplied together. Name it once so they recognise the term later — multiplying local slopes along a path is precisely the chain rule; we just never write the symbols. This chain-of-boxes picture is the mental image to keep.

Do the easy weight first to build confidence. w2 is the last weight, so its path to the loss is short: guess → error → loss. Start with the shared signal, 2×error = 2×0.5 = 1.0. Then the only weight-specific piece: the guess is w2 times h, so if we nudge w2, the guess moves by h = 1 (this is the "slope 1 line" idea — the multiplier IS the other factor, h). Multiply: gradient of w2 = 1.0 × 1 = 1.0. Say what the sign means: it is positive, so increasing w2 would increase the loss — therefore we will decrease w2. Have them note the number 1.0; we use it on the step slide. Everyone can check this in their head.

Now the deeper weight, using the exact same recipe — just more arrows. Start identically with 2×error = 1.0. Then walk backward: the guess is w2 times h, so the guess reacts to h by the factor w2 = 1.5; the hidden unit went through ReLU and h was positive, so the ReLU slope is 1 (it passed the signal through unchanged — if h had been clamped to 0, this factor would be 0 and w1 would get no blame at all); finally the hidden pre-activation is w1 times x, so it reacts to w1 by the factor x = 2. Multiply the whole chain: 1.0 × 1.5 × 1 × 2 = 3.0. That is the gradient of w1. Point out the pattern they can now see: a deeper weight's gradient is the shallower signal times the extra local slopes on the way in. This is literally what loss.backward() automates — reusing the shared front part (the 1.0) for every weight.
Backprop → step · one full update by hand

Take the step: weight − lr × gradient

learning rate lr = 0.1
both weights came down — the guess was too high
Did the loss actually drop? Re-run the forward pass with the new weights:
Loss fell 0.25 → 0.19 in one step. Repeat thousands of times and the guess closes in on the truth.
The payoff — one complete learning step, start to finish, by hand. Recall the update rule: new weight = old weight − lr × gradient. With a learning rate of 0.1: w2 goes 1.5 − 0.1×1.0 = 1.4; w1 goes 0.5 − 0.1×3.0 = 0.2. Both dropped, which is exactly right — the guess was too high and both weights were positive, so lowering them lowers the guess. Now the satisfying check on the right: plug the new weights back through the network and the loss falls from 0.25 to 0.19 after a single step. That is the entire mechanism of deep learning: forward, loss, backward, step — and the loss ticks down. Do a hundred thousand of these and you have a trained network. Note the learning rate's role live: too big a value here would overshoot and the loss could rise instead — connect back to the three bowls. This slide and the quiz share these numbers exactly.
Backprop · autograd checks our work

The exact same example, in PyTorch

import torch

x, y = torch.tensor(2.0), torch.tensor(1.0)   # input, target
w1 = torch.tensor(0.5, requires_grad=True)
w2 = torch.tensor(1.5, requires_grad=True)

h    = torch.relu(w1 * x)     # hidden neuron
pred = w2 * h                 # the guess
loss = (pred - y) ** 2        # squared error
loss.backward()               # backprop → fills .grad

print(pred.item(), loss.item())
print(w1.grad.item(), w2.grad.item())
console output
pred = 1.5   loss = 0.25
w1.grad = 3.0   w2.grad = 1.0
Autograd's numbers are exactly the ones you found by hand: gradients 3.0 (w₁) and 1.0 (w₂). loss.backward() ran the whole backward chain for you.
The reveal that the by-hand walk was not a toy — it is literally what the computer does. Read the script top to bottom: it builds the same tiny net (x = 2, w1 = 0.5, w2 = 1.5, target 1), and the two weights are flagged requires_grad=True, which tells PyTorch to track them for gradients. The forward three lines mirror our slides exactly: hidden via relu, the guess, the squared-error loss. Then the single line loss.backward() runs the entire backward chain and deposits each weight's gradient into its .grad. The printout is the punchline: pred = 1.5 and loss = 0.25 (our forward numbers), and w1.grad = 3.0, w2.grad = 1.0 — the very gradients we multiplied out by hand, to the decimal. Say it plainly: you now understand what autograd computes, so it is never a black box to you. In Lab 1 you will not write this from scratch; you will fill blanks in exactly this shape. (Note the squared error with no one-half is why the start signal was 2×error — PyTorch uses the same convention.)
Backprop · at scale

The same arrows on a whole network = loss.backward()

A real network has thousands of weights. Backprop finds every gradient in one backward sweep — reusing the shared parts of each path, exactly as our 1.0 was reused for both weights.

You will never compute these by hand again. One call — loss.backward() — does the whole sweep for you. But now you know what it is doing.
Zoom out and relieve the anxiety. Our two-weight chain was the hard part conceptually; a real network is just many such chains sharing their front sections. The red path is exactly the calculation we just did, now one thread among thousands. Backprop is efficient because it reuses shared pieces — just as our 2×error = 1.0 was reused for both w1 and w2, the algorithm computes each shared slope once and fans it out. The liberating punchline: you will never do this by hand again — PyTorch's loss.backward() runs the entire backward sweep automatically. But unlike most people who type that line, you now know precisely what it computes: the loss's slope for every single weight, so the optimiser knows which way is downhill. Bridge to the code slide.
Lecture 2 · part four

The PyTorch training loop

everything you just did, in five lines you will recognise
Bring it home. All the intuition now collapses into a tiny, readable block of code — the loop they will fill in blanks on in Lab 1. Set the expectation: you will not memorise syntax, you will recognise the four boxes from slide 3.
Training loop · five steps, line by line

The whole thing, annotated

for epoch in range(20):              # repeat the loop many times

    pred = model(x)                 # 1 · FORWARD   — make a guess
    loss = loss_fn(pred, y)         # 2 · LOSS      — how wrong?

    optimizer.zero_grad()           # 3 · ZERO      — clear old gradients
    loss.backward()                 # 4 · BACKWARD  — backprop the blame
    optimizer.step()                # 5 · STEP      — nudge every weight
Same four boxes from slide 3. The one new habit: zero_grad — PyTorch adds up gradients, so you must wipe last step's before backprop, or they pile up.
Read it top to bottom in plain English, mapping each line to what we built. The for-loop just repeats everything. Line 1, forward: model(x) pushes the input through and returns the guess — Lecture 1's forward pass. Line 2, loss: compare guess to truth, get the one "how wrong" number. Line 3, zero_grad: the only line without an obvious counterpart in our hand-calc — PyTorch accumulates gradients by default, so before each backward we must reset last iteration's to zero, or they add up and corrupt the step; forgetting it is the single most common beginner bug. Line 4, backward: the whole backward sweep from the last section, computing every gradient. Line 5, step: apply weight − lr × gradient to every weight, using the learning rate set on the optimizer. Tell them: in Lab 1 these five lines are exactly the blanks you fill in. The spec's canonical order is forward → loss → zero → backward → step; that is what the quiz asks them to reproduce.
