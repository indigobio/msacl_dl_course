Lecture 4 · Part 1 of 3

Loss function choice

cross-entropy for classification — it punishes confident wrong answers
Part one of three: which loss. One idea to hold: for a yes/no or a category, the network outputs a probability, and the right loss should barely shrug at an unsure guess but come down hard on a confident mistake. That loss is cross-entropy. We build it in three steps — turn scores into probabilities, look at the penalty shape, read one worked number on a real MS call — then name its cousin, focal loss, in one breath before moving to tuning.
Part 1 · loss · step 1 · probabilities

A classifier outputs probabilities that sum to 1

The final layer gives a raw score per class. Softmax squashes them into probabilities — all positive, adding to 1:

Now "the answer" is a confidence in each class, not a single number. Any number of classes works the same way — 2 here, or 30 for species ID from a spectrum; just more terms in the sum.
Keep this light — softmax is a mechanism, not the star, but now we DO show the formula since the instructor asked for it: numerator e^z_i exponentiates one class's raw score (guarantees positive); denominator sums that same exponential over every class (the normalizer that forces the total to 1). Walk it once on the R/S example: exponentiate 2.0 and 0.5, add them, divide each by that sum, land near 0.82/0.18. Then the multi-class touch: nothing about softmax is binary-only — the same sum-and-divide works whether there are 2 classes or 200; a species-ID model just has a longer sum in the denominator. That confidence is exactly what the next slide's loss will grade: a good loss should reward high probability on the TRUE class and punish high probability on a WRONG class. Anchor it in their world — this is precisely the output of a MALDI-TOF resistance classifier like the DRIAMS models in Lab 2.
Part 1 · loss · step 2 · the penalty shape

Penalty = "how surprised were you by the truth?"

Cross-entropy looks only at the probability you assigned to the correct class:

  • said 0.9 and right → barely any penalty
  • said 0.5 — a shrug → moderate penalty
  • said 0.02 — confidently wrong → penalty rockets
That steep left wall is the whole idea: being sure and wrong is the worst thing a classifier can do.
This is the intuition to land, and it is the "penalize confident wrong answers" outcome verbatim. Read the curve from right to left: if you gave the true class a probability near 1, you were right and sure, so the penalty is essentially zero; at 0.5 you were on the fence, so a moderate penalty; as your probability for the truth heads toward zero — meaning you were confidently backing the WRONG class — the penalty shoots up toward infinity. That asymmetry is deliberate and clinically sensible: a lab test that hedges is tolerable, a test that is loudly, confidently wrong is dangerous, and the loss reflects that. Do not write "−log p" on the board as math; just call it "the surprise penalty" and let the shape carry it. Contrast with MSE in one line: MSE would treat 0.9-vs-1 and 0.1-vs-0 as similar-sized misses; cross-entropy blows the confident miss up enormously.

Part 1 · loss · the formula, in plain words

Cross-entropy is just −log(the probability you gave the truth)

the label is 1 for the correct class, 0 for the rest — so the sum keeps only the true class's term

Read it right to left: take the probability you gave the correct class, take its log, flip the sign.

Walk the numbers
ptrue = 1.0 → −log(1.0) = 0  no penalty
ptrue = 0.5 → −log(0.5) = 0.69
ptrue = 0.1 → −log(0.1) = 2.30  confident & wrong
Exactly the surprise curve from two slides ago — now as a formula: high probability on the truth → tiny penalty; near-zero → it explodes.
Rule of thumb: predict a number → MSE; choose a class → cross-entropy. In PyTorch, one line: nn.MSELoss → nn.CrossEntropyLoss. (A relative, focal loss, tilts this toward rare/hard cases — more when we discuss class imbalance.)
Touch the actual formula, but stay intuition-first — no derivation. Put it up and translate every symbol into plain words. The sum runs over the classes; y is the one-hot label — a 1 on the correct class and 0 everywhere else — so every term except the true class's is multiplied by zero and vanishes. What survives is a single term: minus the log of the probability the model assigned to the TRUE class. That's the whole formula: −log(p_true). Now walk the three numbers so the shape is concrete: give the truth probability 1.0 and −log(1.0) = 0, no penalty, you were right and sure; give it 0.5 and the penalty is about 0.69; give it 0.1 — you were confidently backing the wrong class — and the penalty is about 2.30 and still climbing toward infinity as p_true → 0. Point back explicitly: this IS the "surprise" penalty curve from the earlier slide, and 0.11 and 1.20 from the worked MALDI example were just this formula at p = 0.90 and 0.30. Close with the one-line rule of thumb (number → MSE, class → cross-entropy; one-line swap in PyTorch, which even applies softmax internally) and a half-sentence pointer to focal loss, which the quiz explores as a discussion. Bridge to Part 2: right loss in hand — now the knobs that make training converge.

Tuning the knobs

optimizer (Adam) · learning-rate schedule · batch size & the other hyperparameters
Part two of three: the settings you choose to make training converge well. We go in order — how we feed the data (mini-batches), which optimizer updates the weights (Adam), how we shrink the step over time (the schedule), why very deep nets are still hard (vanishing/exploding — a short beat that sets up Lecture 5's ResNet), then the hyperparameters you actually dial: batch size — with its effect on the loss curve — plus learning rate and epochs, and how to search them.
Part 2 · tuning · how the data is fed

Mini-batches: step often, on a handful at a time

Split the data into mini-batches (say 32), and after each batch take one step — don't wait to see all the data.

pick batch 1 → update once
pick batch 2 → update once
… until all batches used = 1 epoch → repeat
This is mini-batch SGD — a data-feeding choice, not an optimizer. 20 batches = 20 updates per epoch (vs. 1 for full-batch): faster, and the noise finds flatter, better-generalizing solutions. The optimizer that acts on each step is next.
This treatment follows the reference deck (Hung-yi Lee, "Deep Learning in one day," mini-batch section). Keep the categorization clean: a "batch" is how many samples you look at before ONE weight step — HOW THE DATA IS FED, not which optimizer you use. Full-batch = use the entire dataset per step: gradient exact, path smooth, but ONE step per pass and everything must fit in memory. Mini-batch = chop into small groups (32, 64, …) and, crucially, UPDATE AFTER EACH GROUP — pick batch 1, take a step; pick batch 2, take a step; when every batch has been used that's one epoch, then repeat (shuffling between epochs). An honest aside from the source: because the loss on each little batch differs, we are not literally minimizing the one total loss each step — the target wobbles — yet it works. Two payoffs the source demonstrates: FASTER — with 20 batches you update 20 times per epoch instead of once; and BETTER — in their experiment mini-batch reached ~0.84 test accuracy vs ~0.12 for no-batch, because the gradient noise nudges the model toward flatter, better-generalizing minima (callback to Lecture 2's local-dip slide). "Stochastic" just means "a bit random." Say explicitly: this is separate from the OPTIMIZER (next slide, Adam), which decides how each step actually updates the weights.
Part 2 · tuning · the optimizer

Adam: a per-weight, adaptive step size

Plain SGD uses one learning rate for every weight. Adam combines two classic ideas:

Adam = adaptive learning rate + momentum
  • Adaptive rate (per weight): smaller past gradients → bigger steps, larger → smaller — tuned automatically from each weight's own gradient history
  • Momentum: like a ball with inertia, keep rolling in a consistent direction — glide through small bumps and past flat spots
A cheap stand-in for the "curvature" idea from Lecture 2 — Adam is the sensible default optimizer and usually "just works."
Follows the reference deck's optimizer section (Hung-yi Lee): the "Recipe of Deep Learning" lists Adaptive Learning Rate and Momentum as two separate tricks, and Adam is literally their combination — "Adam = RMSProp (advanced Adagrad) + Momentum." Give each in plain words. Adaptive rate (the Adagrad/RMSProp idea): each weight gets its OWN learning rate scaled by its recent gradient sizes — where gradients have been small the effective rate grows, where they've been large it shrinks, so flat directions get bigger steps and steep ones smaller steps automatically. Momentum (the physics idea, now drawn literally): picture a monkey sliding down a hillside toward the valley floor (low loss). A PLAIN step only looks at the slope right under its feet — hit a small bump or a flat plateau (gradient ≈ 0) and it just stops there, stuck, even though the true valley is further down. A monkey with MOMENTUM keeps some of its downhill velocity from the steps before, so it carries straight over that same bump without even noticing it, and keeps sliding toward the real bottom. That is exactly what momentum adds to gradient descent: this step's move = mostly the accumulated velocity, nudged a little by the current gradient — it glides through small bumps, coasts across flat spots, and doesn't overreact to the mini-batch noise from the last slide. Tie back to the OPTIONAL curvature slide in Lecture 2: momentum is a cheap, physical stand-in for using the shape of the valley to pick the step. Practice: use Adam (adaptive rate + momentum together) as your default optimizer; it removes most of the pain of hand-tuning the learning rate — though the rate still matters, which is why we schedule it next.
Part 2 · tuning · the learning-rate schedule

Schedule the rate: warmup → hold → decay

Even with Adam, one fixed step size is rarely ideal. A popular modern shape is warmup–stable–decay:

  • Warmup — ramp up gently so early noisy steps don't blow up
  • Stable — hold a high rate to travel fast
  • Decay — drop to tiny steps to settle in
Why hold, then decay? Picture the loss landscape as a river valley: steep mountain walls on both sides, a flat river of real progress along the bottom.
The learning-rate SCHEDULE, the explicit Lecture 2 callback, reframed with the paper's own "river valley" picture (arXiv:2410.05192), which is the piece the instructor specifically asked to add. Panel A is the valley seen in CROSS-SECTION, cut across the river: two steep mountain walls (sharp-curvature directions) with a flat river channel at the bottom (the direction where real optimization progress happens). Panel B tracks the SAME ball's distance from that river over training steps, and lines it up with the three schedule phases. WARMUP: the rate ramps up, so the ball's swing away from the river grows from nothing. STABLE: a high, constant rate keeps the ball bouncing hard between the two mountain walls — big, noisy swings — but underneath that noise it is still creeping along the river, i.e. still making real progress, which is why the loss curve can look choppy here without anything being wrong. DECAY: dropping the rate sharply stops the bouncing almost immediately, and the ball settles into the river itself — this is the moment the loss visibly falls off a cliff in WSD, because the oscillation that was hiding the true progress finally disappears. Contrast briefly with COSINE (mentioned, not drawn here): a smooth, gradually-shrinking rate the whole time, versus WSD's hold-then-drop, which lets you decide how long to train without fixing the schedule in advance. Clarify vs. Adam: Adam adapts the step PER WEIGHT from recent gradients; the schedule scales the OVERALL rate over TIME — they stack. Bridge: optimizer and schedule set, but very deep nets still fight back — why, next.
Part 2 · tuning · why deep nets are harder

Whisper down the lane: the gradient fades or blows up

early-weight gradient = 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 ≈ 0.02  — Lecture 2's "multiply the local effects along the path," now across many layers
A whispered message down a line: after enough layers it arrives as silence (vanishing — the layers nearest the input barely learn) or a scream (exploding). No optimizer fully fixes this — the real fix, ResNet skip connections, is next lecture.
A short but required beat inside tuning — it names a limit the optimizer can't cure and sets up Lecture 5's ResNet. Ground it in Lecture 2 and in the reference deck (Hung-yi Lee, "Vanishing Gradient Problem"). Recall backprop from Lecture 2: a weight's gradient is the PRODUCT of the local slopes along the path from the loss back to that weight — "multiply the local effects along the path." For a deep net that path passes through every layer above the weight, so the gradient is a long product of per-layer factors. Show the multiplication explicitly with the numbers on screen: if each factor is about 0.5 (a sigmoid's slope is at most 0.25, so this is optimistic), then seven layers give 0.5×0.5×…×0.5 = 0.5⁷ ≈ 0.02 — the signal has withered to almost nothing by the time it reaches the EARLIEST layers, so those layers get essentially no blame and barely learn while the layers near the output learn fast (the source's exact point: smaller gradients near the input, larger near the output). That is "vanishing gradients." Flip the factor to about 2 and the product 2⁷ = 128 explodes — updates blow up, weights fly off, loss prints NaN. The "whisper down the lane" image nails it. Mention the partial fixes in a breath — ReLU (slope 1 for positive inputs, so it doesn't squash), good initialization, normalization — then plant the hook: the real cure, the ResNet skip connection that lets the gradient bypass layers, is the headline of Lecture 5.
Part 2 · tuning · the hyperparameters

The knobs you set — tune the learning rate first

Hyperparameters are the settings you choose before training (the weights are what training learns). There are a huge number of them:

depth · width · activation · initialization · optimizer · momentum · dropout rate · weight decay · schedule · batch size · epochs · …

We only tune the few basic ones that matter most:

  • 1. Learning rate — by far the most impactful
  • 2. Batch size — memory & the trade-off, next slides
  • 3. Model size / epochs — capacity & how long
Get the learning rate right and half your problems disappear. Everything else can start at its library default.
Order of attack: learning rate → batch size → model size / epochs.
Set expectations honestly first: the list of things you COULD tune is enormous — network depth and width, which activation, how weights are initialized, the optimizer and its momentum, dropout rate, weight-decay strength, the learning-rate schedule, batch size, number of epochs, and more. Nobody sweeps all of them. The skill is knowing the handful that move the needle, and we deliberately cover only those basics. Define hyperparameter cleanly: a setting YOU pick before training, as opposed to the weights the loop LEARNS. Then the ranking: the learning rate dominates everything (callback to Lecture 2's three bowls and loss-curve diagnosis, and to the schedule two slides back) — get it in the right ballpark before touching anything else; then batch size (its trade-off and loss-curve fingerprint are the next two slides); then model size and number of epochs. Give the order-of-attack line as the practical recipe. Note: regularization strength (dropout, weight decay) is also a hyperparameter, but we meet those as OVERFITTING fixes in Part 3, so we leave them off this tuning list. Bridge: batch size next, and you can see it in the loss curve.
Part 2 · tuning · batch size · the trade-off

Batch size shapes which valley you land in

Small (e.g. 8)
  • noisy gradient, many updates
  • noise → flat minima, generalizes
  • underuses the GPU → slow
Middle (32–128)
  • reasonable gradient estimate
  • efficient on the hardware
  • the usual starting point
Too big (whole set)
  • fewer updates per epoch
  • may not fit in memory
  • sharp minima → worse generalization
The batch-size trade-off — cover BOTH what a bigger batch buys AND the too-big problem (quiz item). Redesigned per feedback to show the actual STEPS in the loss plane, not just the end basins. Read the figure as one shared landscape viewed from above (contour lines = equal loss, like a topographic map): every gradient step is a hop from one contour ring toward the center. LEFT — small batch: each mini-batch gives a noisy, slightly-wrong estimate of the true gradient, so every step is small and points in a slightly different direction — many short, jittery hops that wander into a wide, FLAT basin (a region where loss stays low over a broad area). RIGHT — large batch: averaging over many more examples gives a clean, accurate gradient estimate, so each step is long, confident, and nearly straight — a few big strides that drive straight into a narrow, SHARP basin (loss is low only in a tiny region). Callback to the sharp/flat-minima idea (Keskar et al.): a flat basin is forgiving — a small shift between training data and new data barely changes the loss there, so it generalizes; a sharp basin is unforgiving — the same tiny shift can send the loss climbing the wall, a "generalization gap." Then the three cards below: what a bigger batch buys (a less noisy gradient, better GPU use) and where it fails when TOO big (fewer weight updates per epoch — 1000 samples in batches of 1000 is one step per epoch vs. ~31 at batch 32 — may not fit in a T4's memory (OOM), and this same sharp-minimum effect). Practical rule: start at 32, grow only if training stays stable and validation doesn't suffer. Next slide shows the same story over training TIME, in the loss curve.
Part 2 · tuning · batch size · on the loss curve

You can see the batch size in the loss curve

Same model, two batch sizes:

  • Large batch — a smooth descent (clean gradient), but fewer updates per epoch, so it can plateau higher
  • Small batch — a jagged, noisy descent, but many updates — often reaches a lower, better-generalizing loss
Smoothness is not the goal — where the curve ends up is. A clean curve that stalls high is the too-big fingerprint.
This is the new figure the feedback asked for — batch size's fingerprint on the loss curve. Walk the two traces. The large-batch curve (teal) is smooth because each gradient is a clean, low-noise estimate, so the descent barely wobbles — but with a big batch you take far fewer weight updates per epoch, and that too-clean gradient tends to settle into a sharp minimum, so for the same number of epochs it often FLATTENS OUT HIGHER (the red "gap"). The small-batch curve (amber) is visibly jagged — each noisy mini-batch yanks the loss around — yet because you take many more updates and the noise nudges you toward flatter, better-generalizing minima, it typically reaches a LOWER loss in the end. The lesson to say out loud, and the trap to avoid: a smooth loss curve is not automatically a good one; do not chase smoothness by cranking the batch size, because a clean curve that plateaus high is exactly the too-big fingerprint. Judge a run by where the curve ENDS UP (and by validation loss), not by how pretty it looks. Reinforces the previous slide and directly supports the quiz batch-size item.
Part 2 · tuning · searching combinations

Grid vs. random search: random usually wins

When one knob matters far more than others, random search samples many more values of it for the same budget — so it beats a rigid grid.
Smarter still — Bayesian optimization: not blind sampling. It builds a quick model from your past trials and picks the next settings where a good score looks most likely — exploiting promising regions while still exploring uncertain ones. (Tools: Optuna, Ray Tune.)
Explain the ladder: grid → random → Bayesian. Grid search: pick a few values per hyperparameter and try every combination — tidy but 4 learning rates × 4 batch sizes = 16 runs yet only 4 distinct learning rates ever tested. Random search: same 16-run budget, sample each hyperparameter randomly, so you test 16 different learning rates AND 16 batch sizes; since usually one knob (the learning rate) dominates, sampling it densely — which random does for free, because its points don't line up in columns — finds a better model sooner. Then the key correction the instructor asked for: the state of the art is NOT purely random. Bayesian optimization keeps a running model (a "surrogate") of how settings map to validation score, fit to all the trials you've already run, and uses it to choose the next trial intelligently — it exploits regions that have scored well while still exploring regions it's uncertain about, so it spends its budget where improvement is most likely instead of sampling blindly. Name the everyday tools (Optuna, Ray Tune, scikit-optimize) so they know where to look; the math (Gaussian processes, acquisition functions) is beyond today. In Lab 2 they run a small structured search themselves. Bridge to Part 3: even perfectly tuned, a model can still fail the real test — generalizing to new samples.

This is the diagnostic outcome and maps straight onto quiz Q1 (four curve pairs). Teach the reading protocol: always plot BOTH curves — training loss (teal, on data the model learns from) and validation loss (red dashed, on held-out data it never trains on). Three signatures. Underfitting: both curves stuck high and close together — the model literally cannot fit the data yet, too small/simple or trained too briefly; the fix is MORE capacity or MORE training, not less. Just right: both fall low and stay close — validation follows training down; the target. Overfitting: training loss keeps dropping toward zero while validation loss bottoms out then climbs — the model is memorizing the training set's noise and quirks; the amber dot marks where validation was best. The one number to watch is the GAP: small and stable is healthy, wide and growing is overfitting. Everything in the rest of Part 3 is a tool to shrink that gap.
Part 3 · overfitting · what it means in your lab

Memorizing one instrument's quirks vs. learning the chemistry

An overfit spectra classifier can learn the baseline wobble and detector noise of your one instrument — hitting 99% in-house.

Move it to a second instrument, site, or reagent lot and it collapses: it memorized the machine, not the biology. (This is a real, published failure mode — Lecture 10.)
Your model scores 99% on training data but only 70% on new samples. Turn to a neighbour — 2 min: what is the first thing you would try, and why?
Ground the abstract idea in their reality, then run the assessed think-pair-share (the 💬 from the spec). The mass-spec anchor: a network trained on spectra from a single instrument can achieve near-perfect training accuracy by latching onto that instrument's fingerprint — its particular baseline drift, detector noise, calibration offset — none of which is the actual biological signal. It looks brilliant until you deploy it on another machine or another site's reagent lot, where those quirks differ, and accuracy craters. Foreshadow Lecture 10's cautionary tale (DRIAMS models losing AUROC across sites). Now the prompt: 99% train, 70% new — classic overfitting; give them two minutes. Harvest good answers and validate the whole toolkit coming up: get more/more-varied data (multiple instruments!), add regularization (dropout, weight decay), stop earlier, or simplify the model — and always evaluate on a truly held-out, ideally external, set. No single "right" answer; the point is they now reach for these tools.
Part 3 · overfitting · fixes 1 & 2

More data · and early stopping

More (and more varied) data is the strongest fix — harder to memorize, easier to find the real pattern. Spectra from several instruments beats many from one.

Early stopping: watch validation loss and stop training at its lowest point — keep the model from the moment before it starts memorizing. Free, and always worth doing.
Two fixes, one slide. More data first because it is the most reliable and the least clever: a bigger, more diverse training set is simply harder to memorize, so the network is pushed toward the genuine pattern; emphasize DIVERSE — for spectra, data from several instruments/sites directly attacks the "memorized one machine" failure. When you cannot get more real data, augmentation (Lab 2) and transfer learning (Lecture 8) are cousins of "more data." Then early stopping, read straight off the curve: validation loss falls, bottoms out, and starts rising as overfitting sets in; the vertical amber line marks that bottom, and early stopping simply keeps the model from that best epoch and quits. It costs nothing, needs only a validation split you already have, and is standard practice. Bridge: the next two fixes actively handicap the network so it can't memorize — dropout and weight decay.
Part 3 · overfitting · fix 3 · regularization

Dropout: randomly switch off neurons each step

The net can't lean on any one neuron, so it builds redundant, robust features — like training a committee of smaller networks that vote. At test time, all neurons are back on.
Visualize it with the two panels — same network, two training steps, a different random ~half of the hidden neurons switched off (dashed, X'd out) each time. The mechanism: on every training step, dropout randomly picks some fraction of neurons (say 50%) and temporarily zeroes them, so the network must predict without them; because which neurons vanish changes every step, no single neuron can become a linchpin that memorizes a training quirk, and the network spreads the work across many redundant features. The intuition to say aloud: it is like training a huge committee of smaller sub-networks that share weights, then letting them vote — averaging many views generalizes better than trusting one. Two practical notes: dropout is only ON during training; at test time every neuron is active (outputs scaled so the math balances), so inference is deterministic. And it is one line in PyTorch — nn.Dropout(0.5); typical rates 0.2–0.5. This is fix three; fix four is weight decay, next.
Part 3 · overfitting · fix 4 · regularization

L2 regularization = weight decay

Big weights let the model bend sharply enough to thread through every noisy point exactly — the output becomes the noise. So we penalize large weights:

every step nudges weights toward 0 — the nickname "weight decay"
A spectra net could pin one huge weight on a noisy m/z channel and "memorize" a fluke. Weight decay spreads reliance across many channels → robust.
Frame L2 as weight decay per the instructor note. The idea: add a second term to the loss that grows with the size of the weights (the sum of the squared weights, scaled by a small knob λ). Now minimizing the loss means two things at once — fit the training data AND keep the weights small — and those trade off. Why smaller weights help, made explicit per feedback: the SAME four noisy dots sit in both panels. A network with huge weights has enough "bend" to route its curve through every single dot exactly — at that point the model's output no longer represents the underlying signal at all, it IS the noise, memorized point for point (the red curve, zero training error, useless on a new sample that has different noise). Forcing weights to stay modest removes that bending power, so the curve can only track the broad trend and physically cannot swing over to touch each individual noisy dot (the teal curve) — it ignores the noise and generalizes. The nickname "weight decay" comes from the mechanics: on top of the normal gradient step, every weight is nudged a little toward zero each update, so unused weights quietly decay away. Keep the equation as a picture, not algebra — λ is just "how hard we pull toward zero." In PyTorch it is the weight_decay argument on the optimizer. Note the family resemblance: L2/weight decay is the same "L2" idea, but here it's the regularization term, not a loss — that is why we keep the name L2. Round out: dropout and weight decay are the two everyday regularizers; use them with early stopping and enough data.
