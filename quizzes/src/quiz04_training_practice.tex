% =========================== Q1 ===========================
\textbf{1. Cross-entropy: the right loss, and which model it punishes.}\; {\small Cross-entropy
scores a \emph{classification} by how much probability you put on the \emph{true} class --- being
confidently wrong hurts most; predicting a plain \emph{number} instead uses MSE. For a one-hot truth
the loss is $\mathrm{CE}=-\ln(p_{\text{true}})$ (natural log).}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(a)} You are predicting two things about an isolate. Which loss fits each --- \emph{MSE} or
\emph{cross-entropy}?

\vspace{2pt}
\quad oxacillin MIC, a concentration (a \emph{number}):\; \rb{MSE}{3cm}

\vspace{2pt}
\quad resistant vs.\ susceptible (a \emph{class}):\; \rb{cross-entropy}{3cm}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(b)} The truth is \emph{resistant} (R). Model~A puts probability \textbf{0.8} on R; Model~B
puts only \textbf{0.2} on R. Using $\mathrm{CE}=-\ln(p_{\text{true}})$, compute each loss --- which
model has the \emph{larger} cross-entropy loss, and why?

\work{1.0cm}
\ifsolution\else\blk{\linewidth}\fi

\keybox{(a) A \textbf{number} (the MIC concentration) $\rightarrow$ \textbf{MSE}; a \textbf{class}
(R vs.\ S) $\rightarrow$ \textbf{cross-entropy} (the output is a probability, and being confidently
wrong should hurt most). (b) \textbf{Model~B.} Plug into $\mathrm{CE}=-\ln(p_{\text{true}})$:
Model~A $=-\ln(0.8)=\mathbf{0.223}$; Model~B $=-\ln(0.2)=\mathbf{1.609}$ --- about $7\times$ larger. B
gave the \emph{true} class only $0.2$, so it was confident in the \emph{wrong} answer, and $-\ln(p)$
rockets as the probability on the truth heads toward zero. \emph{Cross-entropy doesn't just grade the
call --- it grades \emph{how confident} you were, so it separates a sure bet from a hedge in a way
plain accuracy can't.}}

\vspace{2pt}
\begin{center}
{\small \textbf{Diagnosis bank:}\;\; underfitting \;$\cdot$\; just right \;$\cdot$\; overfitting
\;$\cdot$\; learning rate far too high (diverging) \\[2pt]
\textbf{Fix bank:}\;\; make the model bigger / train longer \;$\cdot$\; keep it (ship it)
\;$\cdot$\; regularize (dropout / weight decay / early stopping / more data) \;$\cdot$\;
lower the learning rate}
\end{center}

\keybox{(a) \textbf{underfitting $\rightarrow$ bigger model / train longer}: both curves sit high and
close and barely move --- the model is too weak or hasn't trained enough, so it hasn't even fit the
training data yet. (b) \textbf{just right $\rightarrow$ keep it}: both drop low and stay close, with
only a small gap --- validation tracks training down. (c) \textbf{overfitting $\rightarrow$
regularize}: training loss keeps falling toward zero while validation \emph{turns back up} (dot marks
the best epoch) --- the model is memorizing training quirks; add dropout / weight decay, stop early,
or get more/more-varied data. (d) \textbf{learning rate far too high $\rightarrow$ lower it}: the
(training) loss swings wildly and \emph{climbs} --- the steps are overshooting; this is a bad-LR
problem, not a capacity or memorizing problem. \emph{The tell for (c) vs.\ the rest: only in (c) do
the two curves \emph{split apart} --- training good, validation bad.}}

\textbf{3. Diagnose the batch size from the symptom.}\; {\small A colleague retrained the same
spectra classifier and hit two problems. For each, say what it reveals about the batch size and name
\emph{one} change you would make.}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(a)} Batch 1024: it crashes with \texttt{CUDA out of memory} before the first epoch runs.

\work{1.0cm}
\ifsolution\else\blk{\linewidth}\fi

\textbf{(b)} Batch 512: it trains fine, but the loss curve is smooth and \emph{plateaus higher} than
the batch-32 run reached.

\work{1.2cm}
\ifsolution\else\blk{\linewidth}\fi

\textbf{(c)} In one line: why is the smooth curve in (b) not automatically the \emph{better} run?

\work{0.8cm}
\ifsolution\else\blk{\linewidth}\fi

\keybox{(a) The batch is \textbf{too big to fit in GPU memory} $\rightarrow$ \textbf{reduce the batch
size} (or keep it and use gradient accumulation). (b) With a very large batch you take \textbf{far
fewer weight updates per epoch} \emph{and} the too-clean gradient settles into a \textbf{sharp minimum
that generalizes worse}, so the loss stalls high $\rightarrow$ \textbf{lower the batch toward $\sim$32}
(or train more epochs). (c) A \textbf{smooth loss curve is not automatically good} --- judge a run by
\textbf{where it ends up and by validation}, not by how steady the curve looks.}

% =========================== Q4 ===========================
\textbf{4. Vanishing gradients --- multiply the slopes down the chain.}\; {\small From Lecture~2, a
weight's gradient is the \emph{product} of the local slopes along the path from the loss back to that
weight; in a deep net that path runs through \emph{every} layer. A gradient of \textbf{1.0} leaves the
loss and passes back through \textbf{5 layers}, each multiplying it by a local slope of \textbf{0.5}.}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(a)} What gradient reaches the \emph{first} layer?\quad $1.0\times0.5^{5}=$ \rb{$0.03$}{2.2cm}

\work{0.5cm}

\textbf{(b)} Is this \emph{vanishing} or \emph{exploding}?\quad \rb{vanishing}{3cm}

\textbf{(c)} In one line, what does that mean for how the \emph{earliest} layers learn?

\work{1.0cm}
\ifsolution\else\blk{\linewidth}\fi

\keybox{(a) $1.0\times0.5^{5}=0.5^{5}=0.03125\approx\mathbf{0.03}$. (b) \textbf{Vanishing} --- every factor is
below~1, so the product shrinks fast. (c) The gradient reaching the earliest layers is almost zero, so
\textbf{those layers barely update / learn} while layers near the output learn fine --- which is why very
deep \emph{plain} networks are hard to train (the fix, ResNet skip connections, is Lecture~5). If each
slope were instead about~2, the product $2^{5}=32$ would \textbf{explode} and the loss would go to NaN.}

% =========================== Q5 (discussion) ===========================
\textbf{5. Discussion --- a rare but critical class.}\; {\small In a real MALDI-TOF resistance
slice (DRIAMS \emph{S. aureus} + oxacillin), only \textbf{41 of 738} isolates are \emph{resistant} (R)
--- about $6\%$; the other $94\%$ are \emph{susceptible} (S). You train a classifier and it reports
\textbf{94\% accuracy}.}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(a)} Why might that 94\% accuracy be \emph{misleading} here --- what could the model be doing?

\work{1.0cm}
\ifsolution\else\blk{\linewidth}\fi

\textbf{(b)} What would you look at \emph{instead} of overall accuracy to know it actually catches the
resistant cases?

\work{1.2cm}
\ifsolution\else\blk{\linewidth}\fi

\textbf{(c)} You \emph{cannot} collect more resistant isolates this cycle. Of these moves ---
\emph{oversample R}, \emph{undersample S}, \emph{class-weight the loss}, \emph{lower the decision
threshold for R} --- which ones help the model attend to the rare class, and what is the one thing
\emph{none} of them can fix?

\work{1.4cm}
\ifsolution\else\blk{\linewidth}\\[10pt]\blk{\linewidth}\fi

\keybox{(a) A model that simply \textbf{always predicts ``susceptible''} scores $94\%$ accuracy while
catching \emph{zero} of the 41 resistant isolates --- with a rare class, overall accuracy is dominated
by the majority and can hide total failure on the cases that matter most. (b) Look at \textbf{class-specific}
measures: \textbf{sensitivity/recall on the resistant class} (what fraction of true R it catches),
\textbf{precision}, the \textbf{confusion matrix}, and a \textbf{precision--recall curve / PR-AUC}
--- not overall accuracy. (c) \textbf{All four legitimately help} --- oversampling R, undersampling S,
class-weighting, and shifting the threshold all push the model to pay more attention to the 41
resistant cases. But the catch: \textbf{none of them adds new resistant \emph{signal}} --- every one
reuses the same 41 examples --- so the real ceiling still needs \textbf{more / more-varied resistant
data} (more isolates, other sites). \emph{Reweighting $\neq$ new information; all revisited in
Lecture 10.}}
